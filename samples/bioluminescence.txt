Bioluminescence: the ocean's own light show

Below about two hundred metres, sunlight fades to nothing. What remains is a
world lit almost entirely by living things. Roughly three quarters of the
animals in the open ocean make their own light — a process called
bioluminescence — and most of them do it for the same three reasons: to hunt,
to hide, or to talk.

The chemistry is remarkably old-fashioned. An animal mixes a molecule called
luciferin with an enzyme called luciferase. The reaction releases a photon of
blue-green light, which is almost exactly the wavelength that travels
furthest through seawater. It is an astonishing piece of luck: the colour the
ocean transmits best is the colour life learned to make.

The most common system works with a third ingredient, a cofactor, and the
cofactor is a flavin, the same family of molecule that carries light in our own
eyes. The reaction is a single step, fast enough to flash on a millisecond
timeline.

Most deep-sea animals do not make a steady glow. They make a flash. A
grenadier shrimp fires a bright burst that startles whatever was approaching
in the dark. The Hawaiian bobtail squid, a small cephalopod that lives in
sand shallows, uses a cloud of luminous bacteria to dim its own silhouette —
a kind of counter-illumination that erases its shadow. The bobtail squid
signals to its partner with a specific pattern of arm movements, and the
partner answers with its own pattern. The vocabulary is written in light.

Some animals cheat. The bobtail squid does not make light itself; it keeps
bacteria in a specialised organ and borrows their glow. A small fish called
the flashlightfish carries a photophore under its eye that is so bright it
lights the seafloor in front of it. And the cookiecutter shark has evolved
light organs along its flanks that leave a glowing outline in the water, a
lure that looks like a small harmless fish until it is not.

Bioluminescence is also the most common form of communication on Earth by
 sheer numbers of species, and we have only looked at a fraction of them.
New deep-sea cameras that film in ultra-low light are now catching animals
doing things no human has ever seen: signalling in patterns we cannot yet
read, courtship dances lit from within, and escape flashes so coordinated
that a whole school of fish goes dark in the same instant.

Why so much light in a place where there is no light to use? One answer is
simply that it works. A flash of light in total darkness is the only signal
that travels efficiently through water. Light attenuates far more slowly than
sound or chemicals, so in a world where nothing can be seen, a light is a
message that carries.

The other answer is older and stranger. Somewhere in the last few hundred
million years, light ceased to be a rarity and became a tool — and then a
language. The deep sea is not a dark place. It is a place that invented its
own sun.

There is a practical reason to care. Every deep-sea animal we have found that
makes light does it with the same handful of chemistry, and that chemistry is
being used right now in laboratories to read cells and to watch tumours grow.
The luciferase that lights up a shrimp is the same enzyme that lights up a
cancer cell under a microscope. A reef fish and a research lab share a
toolkit.

There is also a darker reason. In the last two centuries we have pulled a
great deal of light out of the ocean — not with lamps, but with nets. The
lights that remain in the deep sea are the ones nobody switched on, and they
are the only ones still there. Studies of the deep ocean have found that
light-emitting organisms are far more common in the water column than anyone
expected, and that the largest animals in the ocean are only the second
brightest thing in it.

The animals themselves do not seem to consider this remarkable. A squid
flashes without ceremony. A fish lights its lure and waits. Somewhere below
the reach of the sun, a whole community goes about its business with lamps
that never go out, speaking to each other in a colour that the surface world
forgot.
